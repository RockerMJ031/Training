The Ladder / six rungs from
chatbox to system

Everyone in this room already uses AI. The difference between a quick answer and a working system is not talent — it is which rung you hold the tool from. Six rungs: chatbox → prompt → context → harness → loop → environment. Most of us stand between rungs 3 and 4. This hour is about that one step — and a look at what the top of the ladder looks like, running in production, today, in this company.

The ladder

Six rungs, one tool. Which rung are you on — and what does the next one buy you?

The jump

Rung 3 → 4: the moment your rules stop being reminders and start being structure.

The vision

Fourteen AI work-lanes, one human. What full scale actually looks like — and the three lessons it teaches.

Where you stand

Same tool, six ways to hold it. Each rung is defined by one question: where does your judgment live? In your head, in a message, in a file, in a structure, in a routine — or in a system that improves itself.

RungNameOne sentenceYou know you're here when…
1ChatboxYou ask, it answers, everything evaporates.…you introduce yourself to the AI every single morning.
2PromptYou learn to ask well — by hand, every time.…your best prompts live in a notes file you paste from.
3ContextYour rules and facts live in files the AI always reads.…the AI knows your role, your tone, your no-gos — without being told.
4HarnessYour most expensive rules become steps that must happen.…the AI can't send, only draft. The send button is yours.
5LoopWork continues and hands over cleanly while you're away.…you start the day by reading one page, not by asking around.
6EnvironmentEvery mistake and every platform update improves the system.…this week's error becomes next week's rule — automatically on the agenda.
INSIGHT 01

The tool is identical on every rung. What changes is how much of your judgment lives outside your head. Climbing the ladder is not about better AI. It is about moving what you know — rules, facts, standards, lessons — out of your memory and into things that persist.

The six rungs · one by one

Rung 1 · The chatbox: everything evaporates

This is where everyone starts, and it works — for one-off questions. You ask, you get an answer, you close the tab. Tomorrow, the AI has never met you.

Watch what a chatbox day looks like for someone answering school emails: paste the email, explain who we are, explain the tone we use, explain what we can and can't promise, ask for a draft. Tomorrow: all of it again. Same explanations, retyped. And because nothing persists, quality is a lottery — the days you explain well, the drafts are good; the days you rush, they aren't.

The ceiling of rung 1: you are the memory. Every session starts from zero, and the quality of the output tracks the effort you spent re-explaining — not the quality of your standards.

Rung 2 · The prompt: asking well, by hand

Rung 2 is the discovery that how you ask changes everything: give the AI a role, the background, an example, the format you want, and what to avoid.

Rung 1 ask
"Reply to this email"

The AI guesses the tone, guesses what we can promise, guesses the sign-off. Three guesses, three chances to be wrong — and you edit for ten minutes.

Rung 2 ask
Role + facts + format + limits

"You draft replies for our school-support team. Warm, professional, British English. Never promise schedule changes — those go to the operations team. Keep it under 150 words. Here's one good example…" The draft comes back nearly sendable.

SAME AI · DIFFERENT ASK · DIFFERENT DAY

But notice what you've built: a small standard. And where does it live? In your clipboard. You paste it every time, you drift a little every time, and a colleague doing the same job has a slightly different version. You've started engineering — by hand.

INSIGHT 02

A good prompt is a draft of a rule. If you've pasted it three times, it wants to be a file. The third paste is the signal. That's the moment rung 3 is calling.

Rung 3 · Context: write it down once

Rung 3 is one move: everything you keep re-explaining goes into a standing-instructions file the AI reads at the start of every session. In Claude Code this file is called CLAUDE.md — but the idea matters more than the name.

What goes inWhat it looks like for our kind of work
Your role & standards"You support the school-operations team. Tone: warm, precise, British English. Sign-off: the team signature, never a personal promise."
Iron rules"Lesson counts and dates come from the schedule system only — never trust the number quoted in an email, including ours."
Where the truth livesA short list: which table is the real source for schedules, for student status, for billing. One source per fact.
VocabularyProduct names, term dates, what "deactivated" actually means here — so the AI speaks your language, not a generic one.
Corrections that must stickEvery time you correct the AI, one line goes in: the rule, and why. Goal: no correction is ever needed twice.

From that day on, the AI opens every session already knowing your role, your tone, your no-gos and where the truth lives. The morning re-introduction is gone. Most of this team is standing here — and it's a good rung. But it has a quiet weakness, and the next rung exists because of it.

INSIGHT 03

The file remembers so you don't have to. Rung 3 is where the AI stops being a stranger every morning. It is also where most people stop climbing — one rung short of the jump that changes everything.

Rung 4 · The harness: rules that can't be broken

Here is the uncomfortable truth about rung 3: a rule in a file is advice. The AI reads it, usually follows it — and sometimes drifts, forgets under a long session, or confidently does the wrong thing at machine speed. For cheap mistakes, that's fine. For expensive ones, advice is not enough.

The jump from rung 3 to rung 4 is this: take your most expensive rules and turn them from words into structure — steps that physically must happen, gates that physically won't open. This is what engineers call a harness, and it needs no code to understand:

1
The draft gate.
The AI can draft an email to a school — it cannot send one. Sending requires you to read the draft and press the button. The rule "always check outgoing mail" stopped being a memo; it became a door.
2
The evidence rule.
Any number in a document — a lesson count, a date, an amount — must arrive with where it was just looked up and when. "The schedule says 5 sessions (checked today 14:02)" passes; a bare "5 sessions" does not.
3
The action whitelist.
The AI may do the things on an approved list. Anything not on the list — deleting records, contacting someone new, changing a schedule — is not "discouraged". It is not available.
The reminder
"Please always double-check the schedule before replying"

Lives in a file. Works until the one busy afternoon it doesn't — and the wrong lesson count goes to a school with our signature under it. Then everyone is reminded harder. Reminders don't compound; they fade.

The machine
A step that blocks the reply until the schedule was actually opened

The draft simply cannot be produced without the fresh lookup attached. Nobody remembers anything. The busy afternoon behaves exactly like the calm one.

A RULE THAT RUNS · NOT A RULE THAT HOPES

Which rules deserve promotion? Not the style ones — the expensive ones. Ask of every rule in your file: what does it cost when this is broken once? A clumsy sentence costs an edit. A wrong number sent to a school, a promise we can't keep, a student's personal detail in the wrong place — those are harness material.

INSIGHT 04

A rule you must remember is a hope. A rule that runs is a guarantee. This is the jump this training is about: find your three most expensive rules, and promote them from paper to structure. Everything else can stay advice.

Rung 5 · The loop: work that survives your absence

Rungs 1–4 assume you are present. Rung 5 asks: what happens to the work when you leave — and what greets you when you return? Two routines, five minutes each, change everything.

1
The sign-off ritual (end of day).
Before finishing, the AI writes a short close-out: what got done (the finished things, not the effort) · what's still open and who it's waiting on · tomorrow's first move. Written at close, while the day is still in memory — not reconstructed in the morning.
2
The morning page (start of day).
You begin by reading yesterday's close-outs — one page — instead of asking around or re-deriving where things stood. Decisions that need you are at the top, each with "what unblocks if you answer".

Why write at close and not in the morning? Because AI sessions have a failure humans don't: when a session's memory fills up, it compresses — and un-written judgment is simply gone. We learned this the real way: one work-lane lost its session mid-week, rewrote its notes from what survived, and the "lessons learned" section came back empty. The work existed; the judgment had evaporated.

The rung-5 principle: memory lives in the system, not in the session. Anything worth keeping is written down before the session ends — because the session will end, and not always politely.
INSIGHT 05

If it isn't written down before the session ends, it never happened. The close-out ritual is five minutes of writing that saves an hour of morning archaeology — and it is the only defence against a kind of amnesia humans never had to plan for.

Rung 6 · The environment: a system that improves itself

The top rung is quiet. You stop improving the system by willpower, and give it two suppliers that never run dry: your mistakes, and the platform's releases.

1
Supplier one: every error becomes one line.
When something goes wrong, the fix isn't "be more careful" — it is one written line: what happened, why, and how to judge it next time. The line goes into the shared file. The same mistake, made twice, means the line wasn't written — not that someone forgot.
2
Supplier two: the weekly upstream check.
Once a week we have Claude Code check on itself: read its own release notes and the best-practice posts from its makers, and answer one question — "which part of what we built by hand does the tool now do natively?" Whatever is covered gets uninstalled. The tool maintains the tool; a human approves.
What "environment engineering" means: the continuous upkeep of everything below — the context files, the harness gates, the routines — driven by evidence, never by mood. The system only changes when an error exposes a gap, or a release makes a piece redundant.
INSIGHT 06

You don't maintain the system. Its errors and their releases maintain it — you approve. This is the rung where the ladder stops being your project and becomes your environment: something that tends itself, and gets a little harder to break every week.

The vision · What full scale looks like

Fourteen lanes, one human

Everything so far scales down to one person and one AI. Here is what it looks like scaled up — not a slide about the future: this runs in our company today, built by the AI architect who works alongside these lanes.

We run about fourteen parallel AI work-lanes — each a long-lived Claude session owning one page or one part of the product. Above them sit two special AI roles: an orchestrator (assigns work, reviews, merges) and an architect (designs, and triages every incoming request). The human count is one: a product owner who describes what's needed in plain words, approves plans, and accepts results. And one thing never moves: no AI can publish to the live product. The publish button is human, by design.

How a request enters the system

You don't need to classify your own request — plain language is enough. Every request becomes one row in a shared task table, and the architect sorts it within the hour using a fixed seven-question checklist. The questions don't ask "how big is this?" — they ask "if this goes wrong, who else gets hit?" A five-line change can be highest-risk; an 800-line one can be routine.

Sorted asMeaningWhat happens
A · Blast radiusTouches shared foundations, interfaces between parts, or things many lanes depend onArchitect writes a one-page plan first — boundaries, contracts, what it deliberately will not do. Human approves, then a lane builds inside the fence.
B · RoutineOne lane's local workAssigned directly — but the working lane runs a three-question self-check before starting; any hit escalates it back to A. That's the safety net for mis-sorting.
C · TrivialDocumentation, or a fix whose cause is already known and touches nothing sharedJust done. But if it turns out to touch anything shared — stop and escalate.

Two anonymised true stories of why the checklist beats gut feel: a request that looked like a small bug fix was actually a change to how two parts of the system talk — sorted casually, the change went in and nobody told the other side; only a safety check caught it. And a request to "add colours to the calendar" looked purely cosmetic — but colours are governed by a site-wide design rulebook with its own automated guard, and a casual change would have bounced straight off it. The checklist knew. Intuition didn't.

The architect's power is what it may not do

  • Proposes, never decides — every plan needs a human yes before it counts.
  • Never builds — construction belongs to the lanes; the architect only holds the acceptance line.
  • Its errors are public record — when a plan is proven wrong by reality, the reversal is logged, reviewed weekly, and the lesson joins the shared rulebook. It has happened. Nothing was deleted.
INSIGHT 07

Boundaries aren't distrust. They give every mistake exactly one owner and one repair path. Fourteen agents with fences beat fourteen agents with freedom — because when something breaks, "whose change was it, and where's the fix?" has a one-line answer.

Staying aligned: memory lives in the system

The hard problem with fourteen agents: they can't see each other — and each can lose its memory without warning. So every alignment mechanism obeys one principle you already met at rung 5: memory lives in the system, not in the session. A day runs as a closed loop:

1
Sign-off, every lane, every day.
Before closing, each lane re-checks its facts live (yesterday's status quoted from memory doesn't count), writes a fixed-format daily note — done · open-and-waiting-on-whom · problems with evidence · new lessons from real errors only — then verifies the note actually saved. Real incident: a lane once "wrote" its note to a file that silently failed to save.
2
One morning page for the human.
A read-only summary pulls all fourteen notes plus the task table into one page: decisions needing the human first (each with "answering this unblocks…"), yesterday's numbers, cross-lane warning signs, and who didn't report. Aligning with fourteen agents costs ten minutes.
3
The weekly up-cycle.
The best "new lessons" from daily notes get numbered into the shared rulebook; process complaints (only the ones with a real example) change the shared routine files. Real case: a lane reported two rules contradicting each other — next day the routine file carried the fix, for everyone. The agent found it; the file fixed it; the team inherited it.
Merged is not live. A task's final state is not "the work is done" — it is "someone verified, with evidence, that it works where users are."
A guard must prove it guards. Every automated check must once demonstrate that it actually turns red when someone breaks the thing on purpose. An alarm that never rang in a drill is not an alarm.
A stale status is worse than none. Status older than a working day counts as expired — write "idle" rather than leave yesterday's state up, because a colleague who reads it won't ask you again.
The red lines. No AI output ever contains passwords, keys, or students' personal information — and anything published is assembled from an explicit allow-list. (A deny-list once let something through. Allow-lists don't have that failure mode.)
INSIGHT 08

One kind of fact, one home. A status lives on the board; detail lives in the note; rules live in the rulebook — and nothing lives in two places, because two homes for one fact means they disagree within a week and an agent reads the wrong one with full confidence.

Three lessons to take home

Strip away the terminology and the whole system teaches three things — and all three apply whether you run one AI or fourteen.

1
Your leverage is at the demand end.
Saying clearly what you want, approving the plan, and personally checking the result moves the outcome more than watching the AI work ever will. The hour you spend sharpening the ask is worth five spent supervising.
2
Any number of AIs — one source of truth per fact.
One status board, one rulebook, one place where each kind of fact lives. Copies drift, and an AI will trust the stale copy exactly as confidently as the fresh one.
3
Changing the process is cheaper than changing anyone.
With AI teammates, a rule written into a shared file binds everyone tomorrow. Every incident that becomes a written line makes the whole system harder to break — that is how it gets better on its own.
"Manage the demand, not the typing. Keep one home per fact. Turn every mistake into a line in a file — and the system improves itself."

Start tomorrow: three smallest steps

Nothing above needs a project or a budget. Three moves, in order — each one climbs you a rung.

  • ① Today — climb to rung 3: create your standing-instructions file. Three parts: your role and tone · your three iron rules · where the truth lives for the facts you use daily. Thirty minutes, and tomorrow the AI already knows you.
  • ② This week — make the jump to rung 4: pick your single most expensive action (for most of us: anything that leaves the building — an email, a document, a number) and put a gate on it: the AI drafts, you approve, only then it goes. One rule, promoted from paper to structure.
  • ③ Tonight — borrow from rung 5: end the day by having the AI write a three-line close-out: what got done · what's open and who it waits on · tomorrow's first move. Read it tomorrow before anything else. That's the loop, in miniature.

That's the hour. The ladder doesn't ask you to become technical — it asks you to move what you already know out of your head and into files, gates and routines. Same tool. Different rung. Different job.
